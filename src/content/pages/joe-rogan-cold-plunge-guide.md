Joe Rogan's cold-plunge routine looks intense because **it is intense**. He has years of exposure to very cold water and talks about temperatures that would make most first-time users climb straight back out.

That does not mean you need to copy the number on his chiller.

> **The useful version:** get the water cold enough that you have to deliberately control your breathing, stay in for a short, repeatable period, and get out before technique turns into a toughness contest. Consistency matters more than proving you can tolerate 37°F.

## Joe Rogan's cold plunge routine at a glance

| Question | Practical answer |
|---|---|
| **Temperature Rogan has discussed** | Roughly mid-30s to low-40s °F |
| **Typical session length** | A few minutes |
| **Beginner target** | Usually much warmer: around 50–60°F is plenty cold to start |
| **Best time** | Morning, separate recovery session, or away from hypertrophy-focused lifting |
| **Main immediate effect** | Strong cold-shock / sympathetic response and reduced soreness perception |
| **Biggest mistake** | Going too cold, too long, too soon |
| **Do you need an expensive tub?** | No. Expensive systems mostly improve convenience, filtration and temperature control |

## What Rogan actually seems to get from cold exposure

Rogan talks about cold exposure less like spa recovery and more like **controlled discomfort**.

That framing makes sense. The first thing cold water does is not “heal” you. It stresses you. Your breathing changes, your blood vessels constrict, your heart rate and blood pressure respond, and every part of your brain tells you to get out.

The skill is staying calm enough to manage that response.

That is probably why people often describe feeling alert afterward. You have just created a very obvious sympathetic nervous-system event. You do not need a mystical explanation for the fact that you feel awake.

## How cold does Joe Rogan go?

Across JRE clips and conversations, Rogan has talked about using water in roughly the **mid-30s to low-40s °F**.

That is very cold.

If you are new, 55°F can feel savage. Even 60°F can produce an involuntary gasp and rapid breathing when you first get in. The idea that “real” cold exposure starts below 40°F is internet machismo, not a physiological requirement.

### A smarter temperature progression

| Experience | Water temperature | Session idea |
|---|---:|---|
| **First few sessions** | 55–60°F | 30–90 seconds |
| **Comfortable beginner** | 50–55°F | 1–2 minutes |
| **Intermediate** | 45–50°F | 1–3 minutes |
| **Experienced** | Below 45°F | Only if you already tolerate cold well and can stay controlled |

These are not medical prescriptions. They are a practical way to avoid the stupidest version of cold plunging: buying a chiller, setting it to 37°F on day one and turning the whole thing into survival.

## How long does Joe Rogan stay in?

Usually a few minutes, depending on the session and temperature.

The important relationship is simple:

**Colder water = less time needed.**

You do not earn bonus points for staying in until your hands stop working properly. If your goal is a repeatable cold stimulus, the session should end while you are still in control.

A clean beginner goal is to get in, settle your breathing, stay composed for a short period and get out intentionally.

## What cold plunging is actually good for

Cold-water immersion has a few reasonably defensible uses. It also has a lot of claims attached to it that get way ahead of the evidence.

### 1. Reducing soreness after hard training

This is the most practical athletic use.

Cold-water immersion can reduce perceived muscle soreness and may help short-term recovery when you need to perform again soon. If you have a tournament, multiple training sessions or back-to-back competition days, that can matter.

The catch is that “feeling less sore tomorrow” and “maximizing adaptation over months” are not always the same goal.

### 2. Feeling alert

This one is not complicated. Cold water is an acute stressor. It produces a strong arousal response.

If you stumble into a plunge in the morning, control the panic-breathing response and get out two minutes later, you are probably not going back to bed.

That is a perfectly legitimate reason to like it.

### 3. Practicing composure under discomfort

This is harder to quantify, but it is probably the part Rogan likes most.

Cold exposure gives you immediate feedback. You either control your breathing and stay calm or you do not. Used sensibly, that can become a little daily practice in not immediately obeying discomfort.

That is a psychological training idea, not a claim that cold water cures anxiety.

## The dopamine claim: what is real and what gets exaggerated

You have probably seen some version of this online:

**“Cold plunging increases dopamine 250% for hours.”**

That is way more precise than most people realize.

Cold stress clearly affects catecholamines and the sympathetic nervous system. But turning specific findings from cold-exposure research into a guaranteed consumer promise about your exact dopamine level after a two-minute backyard plunge is a leap.

The practical thing you can actually observe is simpler: **cold exposure can make you feel intensely awake and activated.** That does not require pretending you have a live dopamine meter in your brain.

## The muscle-growth problem nobody should hide

If you lift specifically to get bigger and stronger, timing matters.

Repeated cold-water immersion **immediately after resistance training** may reduce some of the signaling and longer-term adaptations involved in hypertrophy and strength development.

That does not mean cold plunges “kill gains.” It means recovery tools can have tradeoffs.

| Your priority | Better move |
|---|---|
| **Maximize hypertrophy** | Put the plunge at another time of day or on non-lifting days |
| **Need to compete/train again soon** | Immediate cold may be worth the recovery tradeoff |
| **Morning alertness** | Plunge in the morning, train later |
| **General wellness habit** | Keep it separate from your most important strength sessions |

If you are lifting three times per week and cold plunging every morning, this is easy to solve. You do not have to choose one forever.

## Cold plunge before or after sauna?

Rogan often combines **sauna + cold**.

The appeal is obvious: intense heat, then intense cold, and sometimes repeat. It feels dramatic and leaves many people feeling great.

But there is no rule saying you have to alternate them to get value from either one.

If you want to mimic the JRE-style ritual:

1. Sauna first.
2. Cool down briefly.
3. Cold plunge for a controlled short exposure.
4. Repeat only if you tolerate it well and have time.

## What cold plunge should you buy?

This is where the category gets ridiculous fast.

A cold plunge has one core job: **hold water cold enough for you to use it consistently**.

Everything after that is convenience.

### Option 1: bathtub + ice

**Best for:** figuring out whether you actually like this before buying equipment.

Pros:

- almost no upfront cost
- no dedicated space
- works immediately

Cons:

- buying ice gets annoying
- temperature changes every session
- not convenient enough for most daily users

### Option 2: insulated tub + ice

**Best for:** low-cost home setup.

An insulated plunge tub holds temperature much better than a regular bathtub. You still deal with ice, drainage and sanitation, but it is a huge step up in convenience without entering luxury-plunge pricing.

[Browse insulated cold plunge tubs on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=insulated+cold+plunge+tub)

### Option 3: tub + chiller

**Best for:** people who know they will use it several times per week.

This is the sweet spot for serious home use. Set the temperature, keep the water filtered, open the lid and get in.

What matters:

- chiller power appropriate for your climate
- actual filtration
- easy sanitation
- insulation
- simple drainage
- replacement parts and warranty

[Browse cold plunge chillers on Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=cold+plunge+water+chiller)

### Option 4: premium all-in-one plunge

**Best for:** convenience, appearance and people who do not care about the price premium.

Premium tubs can be great. They can also cost several thousand dollars to do a job an insulated tub and competent chiller can do for much less.

Pay more for **build quality, filtration, quiet operation, insulation and service**. Do not pay more because an app sends you a badge for sitting in cold water.

## Features that actually matter

Ignore most of the lifestyle marketing. Look for:

- **temperature stability**
- **good insulation**
- **water filtration**
- **sanitation**
- **easy drain access**
- **enough interior room to submerge comfortably**
- **a cover** if it lives outside
- **serviceable parts**
- **real warranty support**

A beautiful tub full of gross water is a bad cold plunge.

## Cold plunge vs. ice bath vs. cold shower

| Method | Best thing about it | Biggest downside |
|---|---|---|
| **Powered cold plunge** | Consistent and easy | Expensive |
| **Ice bath** | Cheap to start | Ice and setup become tedious |
| **Cold shower** | Free and convenient | Less consistent whole-body cooling |
| **Natural cold water** | Free and intense | Safety, currents, weather and uncontrolled temperature |

A cold shower is not “fake.” If the goal is to practice tolerating cold and wake yourself up, it is a perfectly reasonable place to begin.

## Safety: do not turn this into a toughness contest

Cold water can produce a rapid **cold-shock response**, including gasping, hyperventilation and substantial cardiovascular stress.

Basic rules:

- do not start with near-freezing water
- do not hyperventilate or do breath-hold exercises in the water
- do not plunge intoxicated
- use a setup you can exit easily
- beginners should not plunge alone
- get out for chest pain, faintness, confusion, severe dizziness or loss of coordination
- people with cardiovascular disease, fainting history or other relevant conditions should ask a clinician before deliberate cold exposure

The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/news/2022/12/05/youre-not-a-polar-bear-the-plunge-into-cold-water-comes-with-risks) has a useful overview of why sudden cold-water immersion deserves more respect than social media sometimes gives it.

## Bottom line

Joe Rogan's near-freezing plunges are **an experienced person's version of the practice**, not the minimum effective dose.

Start warmer. Get your breathing under control. Stay in briefly. Use it for a reason.

If you love it enough to do it several times per week, then a chiller starts to make sense. If you try it six times and hate every second, congratulations: you just saved yourself a few thousand dollars.
