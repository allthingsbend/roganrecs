Joe Rogan has helped make deliberate cold exposure mainstream, but the useful part is not copying the coldest temperature he can tolerate. It is understanding **what cold water can do, what it cannot do, and how to use it without sabotaging another goal**.

> **Quick takeaway:** Rogan commonly uses very cold water for a few minutes. For most people, starting warmer and shorter is smarter. You do not need near-freezing water to create a strong cold stimulus.

## Joe Rogan's cold-plunge routine

Across JRE episodes and social clips, Rogan has described regular cold-water immersion as part of his recovery and resilience routine. His exact temperature and duration have changed, but he often talks about water in roughly the **mid-30s to low-40s °F** and exposures lasting a few minutes.

Those numbers should be read as **his tolerance**, not a prescription. A first-time user can get a dramatic cold response at 50–60°F.

## What cold plunging actually does

The immediate effects are clear: cold water rapidly constricts peripheral blood vessels, increases sympathetic nervous-system activity and triggers the cold-shock response. Breathing and heart rate can jump quickly, especially during the first minute.

The most established practical benefit for athletes is **reduced perceived soreness and improved short-term recovery** after demanding exercise. That is useful when performance tomorrow matters more than maximizing training adaptation today.

The evidence is weaker for broad claims such as 'detoxing,' permanently boosting testosterone or producing major fat loss by itself.

## The muscle-growth tradeoff

This is one of the most important details that many cold-plunge guides leave out.

Research on repeated cold-water immersion after resistance training suggests that doing it **immediately after lifting, over and over**, may reduce some signaling and long-term adaptations related to muscle growth and strength.

That does not make cold plunging bad. It means the timing should match the goal.

| Goal | Better approach |
|---|---|
| Reduce soreness before another competition/session | Cold immersion soon after training can make sense |
| Maximize hypertrophy | Avoid making immediate post-lift cold immersion a habit |
| General wellness / alertness | Use cold exposure at another time of day |
| Morning energy | A short cold shower or plunge may be enough |

## How cold should the water be?

There is no magic Rogan number.

A practical progression:

- **Beginner:** 55–60°F for 30–90 seconds
- **Intermediate:** 45–55°F for 1–3 minutes
- **Advanced:** lower temperatures only if you are already adapted and can control your breathing

The colder the water, the shorter the session usually needs to be.

## Safety matters more than toughness

Cold-water immersion can produce a strong cardiovascular response. Never treat breath-holding contests or staying in until numbness as a badge of honor.

Basic rules:

- enter and exit somewhere you cannot slip or become trapped
- do not plunge alone if you are inexperienced
- avoid combining deliberate cold exposure with alcohol
- get out for chest pain, severe dizziness, confusion or loss of coordination
- people with cardiovascular disease or relevant medical conditions should get clinician guidance first

## Cold plunge vs. ice bath vs. cold shower

**Cold plunge:** reusable tub with temperature control; most convenient if used several times per week.

**Ice bath:** inexpensive upfront but requires repeated ice and more setup.

**Cold shower:** cheapest option and plenty uncomfortable, though the whole body is not submerged and the stimulus is less consistent.

For a beginner, a cold shower is a perfectly reasonable way to learn whether you even like deliberate cold exposure before spending thousands on equipment.

## Bottom line

Joe Rogan's cold-plunge routine is an example of **advanced tolerance**, not a beginner template. Start warmer, keep sessions short, control your breathing and decide what you are using cold exposure for.

If muscle growth is the goal, separate cold plunging from lifting. If short-term soreness reduction or alertness is the goal, cold water can be a useful tool.
