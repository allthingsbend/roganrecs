Most Rogan fans know about the comedy, the UFC and the elk. Fewer know he's a serious pool player, the kind who knows cue makers by name and talks about them the way car people talk about engine builders.

He's posted a custom cue made for him by **Barry Szamboti**, calling Szamboti one of the true legends of the craft. And on JRE he's talked about the **Balabushka**, the most mythologized pool cue ever made, and where the fact ends and the fiction starts.

Here's what those cues are, what they cost, and what someone who's just getting into pool should buy instead.

> **The short version:** Rogan's cue that we can verify is a **custom Szamboti**. Those, and the Balabushkas he's talked about, are collector pieces that cost thousands to tens of thousands of dollars. If you want to play, get a straight two-piece cue like the [McDermott Lucky L9](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0725JVWPS), good chalk like [Kamui Roku](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FMQT9XNX), and a case. That's the whole kit.

## The cue Rogan posted: a Barry Szamboti

Rogan shared a custom cue on X that he said was made for him by Barry Szamboti. The name carries weight in pool for a reason.

- **Gus Szamboti** started making cues in 1969 after working as a draftsman at RCA. He was obsessive about wood and threw out most of what he inspected.
- He made somewhere around **1,200 to 1,300 cues** in his life. Some took more than a year to finish.
- He supplied the blanks George Balabushka used from 1971 to 1975, the stretch collectors call the "Szamboti era."
- After Gus died in 1988, his son **Barry** took over and kept the standards. Gus is in the International Cuemakers Association Hall of Fame.

Original Gus Szamboti cues commonly trade in the high four to five figures. A new Barry Szamboti is a custom order with a wait. Neither is something you'll find on Amazon, and anything listed as one there should make you suspicious.

## The Balabushka legend

**George Balabushka** (1912 to 1975) made cues for about 16 years and is routinely called the Stradivarius of cue makers. His output was small, roughly 1,000 to 1,200 cues, which is part of why the name became a legend.

The other part is Hollywood. In *The Color of Money* (1986), Tom Cruise's character is handed a Balabushka and treats it like a holy relic. The scene made the name famous outside pool halls. The irony: **the cue in the movie wasn't a real Balabushka.** The production used a replica rather than risk an original.

What that means for buyers:

- **Verified originals sell for tens of thousands of dollars.** One sold for $45,000 back in 1994.
- **Counterfeits are common.** Provenance is everything.
- **Licensed replicas exist** and have been made with the family's permission since the 1980s. They're a reasonable way to own the look, but they're not collector pieces.

## What you should actually play with

A custom cue doesn't make anyone better at pool. Straightness, a consistent tip and a solid joint matter far more than inlays. For someone getting into the game:

### What to look for in any cue

1. **Roll it on a flat table.** If it wobbles, it's warped. Don't buy it.
2. **Weight around 18 to 19 oz** suits most players. Heavier isn't better.
3. **A 13 mm tip** is standard for pool. Tips wear out and get replaced; the shaft is what you're really buying.
4. **Avoid one-piece bar cues** for your own use. They're fine for a pub. They're not a cue you build a stroke on.

## When a custom cue makes sense

If you play several nights a week, you've already worn out a couple of playing cues and you care about the craft, a custom cue from a respected maker is a reasonable thing to want. It's a piece of handmade work you'll keep for life. Just buy it from the maker or a trusted dealer, and treat any "vintage Balabushka" deal that seems too good the way you'd treat a cheap Rolex.
