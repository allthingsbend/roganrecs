Andrew Huberman has discussed a long list of supplements across Huberman Lab episodes, interviews and Q&As. But there is an important distinction that gets lost on many supplement-stack pages: **a supplement appearing on his podcast does not necessarily mean he takes it every day, recommends it to everyone, or considers the evidence equally strong.**

This guide separates the supplements Huberman has publicly said he uses from compounds he has discussed more generally, and it adds the context that matters: why he uses them, the doses he has mentioned, how often he has described taking them, and where the evidence or safety picture is less certain.

> **Quick takeaway:** Huberman's overall approach is not "take everything." He repeatedly emphasizes behavior, nutrition, sleep and exercise first, then using supplements selectively for a specific goal.

## The most important thing to understand about Huberman's supplement philosophy

Huberman's supplementation episode lays out a useful hierarchy: first identify the outcome you actually want, then ask whether behavior, nutrition or prescription care is the more appropriate tool before adding supplements. In other words, he does not present supplements as replacements for sleep, exercise, food quality or medical treatment.

That distinction is especially important with his content because he has discussed hundreds of molecules. Some are part of his personal routine. Others are examples from research, guest conversations or mechanistic discussions.

Source: [Huberman Lab — Developing a Rational Approach to Supplementation for Health & Performance](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/developing-a-rational-approach-to-supplementation-for-health-and-performance)

## Huberman's sleep supplements

Sleep is probably the area most associated with the "Huberman supplement stack." His best-known combination is magnesium, apigenin and L-theanine, generally taken before bed.

### Magnesium threonate or magnesium bisglycinate

Huberman has repeatedly discussed magnesium threonate and magnesium bisglycinate as forms he prefers in the context of sleep. In his Sleep Toolkit episode, magnesium threonate is one of the core sleep supplements discussed alongside apigenin and theanine.

A commonly cited version of his personal protocol has included roughly **145 mg of magnesium threonate 30–60 minutes before sleep**. Be careful when comparing labels: some list the weight of the entire magnesium-threonate compound while others list elemental magnesium, which are not the same number.

Why people use it: magnesium is involved in nerve signaling, muscle function and many enzymatic processes. But the claim that magnesium reliably transforms sleep for everyone is stronger than the evidence. Benefits may be more noticeable in people with inadequate magnesium intake or certain sleep problems.

Source: [Huberman Lab — Sleep Toolkit](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/sleep-toolkit-tools-for-optimizing-sleep-and-sleep-wake-timing)

### Apigenin

Apigenin is a flavonoid found naturally in chamomile and other plants. Huberman has commonly discussed **about 50 mg before bed** as part of his sleep protocol.

The rationale is relaxation and reduced mental activation before sleep. That said, apigenin has far less high-quality human sleep research behind it than the popularity of the "Huberman sleep stack" might imply.

### L-theanine

Huberman has discussed **roughly 100–400 mg of L-theanine** before sleep. L-theanine is an amino acid found in tea and is commonly used for relaxation without heavy sedation.

He has also pointed out an important individual-response issue: some people report unusually vivid dreams from theanine, and for those people it may make sleep feel worse rather than better.

### Myo-inositol, glycine and GABA

Huberman has also discussed myo-inositol, glycine and GABA in the context of sleep. These are better thought of as **situational tools**, not mandatory pieces of one fixed nightly stack.

Myo-inositol has been discussed particularly for people who wake in the middle of the night and have trouble falling back asleep. Huberman has also said he does not necessarily take the exact same sleep supplements every night.

### What about melatonin?

Huberman has generally been cautious about routine melatonin supplementation in healthy adults. In his sleep episodes, he has emphasized that commercial supplements often contain much larger doses than endogenous nighttime melatonin levels would suggest and that melatonin is not his default recommendation for improving normal sleep quality.

That does not mean melatonin has no medical uses. It can be useful for specific circadian-rhythm situations, and pediatric or clinical use should be handled with an appropriate healthcare professional.

## Creatine: one of the strongest entries on the list

Creatine monohydrate is one of the more evidence-backed supplements Huberman discusses.

He has explained that **5 grams per day** is a common daily dose and that he personally takes closer to **10 grams per day** because he is around 100 kg / 220 lb. He has also emphasized that a loading phase is not required; taking a consistent daily dose will raise tissue creatine stores over time.

Creatine's strongest evidence is for muscle strength, power and training performance. Huberman also highlights research into brain creatine and cognition, especially in people who consume little or no meat and in conditions where brain-energy demand is high.

Source: [Huberman Lab — AMA #11: Improve Task Switching & Productivity and Reduce Brain Fog](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/ama-11-improve-task-switching-productivity-and-brain-fog)

Source: [Huberman Lab — Nutrients for Brain Health & Performance](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/nutrients-for-brain-health-and-performance)

For most people looking for a simple, well-studied performance supplement, **plain creatine monohydrate** is the form with the deepest evidence base. More expensive creatine variants have not consistently shown superior real-world outcomes.

## Alpha-GPC for focus and training

Alpha-GPC is one of the clearest examples of a supplement Huberman has described using personally for focused work and workouts.

In his Focus Toolkit episode, he says he has found **300–600 mg** useful before a work bout or workout, with **300 mg often being enough for him**. He has described taking it roughly 10–20 minutes before deep cognitive work and, elsewhere, using it several times per week rather than necessarily every day.

Alpha-GPC provides choline and can raise acetylcholine signaling, which is involved in attention and learning.

Source: [Huberman Lab — Focus Toolkit](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/focus-toolkit-tools-to-improve-your-focus-and-concentration)

There is an important caution here. Huberman himself has discussed observational research linking high or chronic Alpha-GPC exposure with stroke risk, while also noting that the data are not definitive. That makes it a poor candidate for the simplistic "more is better" approach.

Source: [Huberman Lab — Optimize & Control Your Brain Chemistry](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/optimize-and-control-your-brain-chemistry-to-improve-health-and-performance)

## L-tyrosine and other focus compounds

L-tyrosine is a precursor used to make catecholamines such as dopamine and norepinephrine. Huberman has discussed it as a **situational focus tool**, particularly when cognitive demand is high.

The research is more convincing for maintaining cognitive performance under stress, cold exposure or sleep deprivation than for turning a well-rested healthy person into a dramatically better thinker.

That is a recurring theme throughout this guide: a mechanistically interesting compound is not automatically a high-value daily supplement.

## Omega-3 fatty acids

Huberman has frequently discussed omega-3 fatty acids, especially EPA and DHA, in the context of cardiovascular and brain health.

Omega-3s are among the more established nutritional supplements, but the evidence depends on the outcome. Prescription-strength omega-3 products can meaningfully lower high triglycerides. Evidence for broad claims such as "better cognition in every healthy adult" is much less definitive.

For someone who regularly eats fatty fish, supplemental needs may be different from someone who rarely consumes marine omega-3s.

When comparing products, focus on the actual grams of **EPA + DHA**, not simply the total number of milligrams of "fish oil" printed on the front of the bottle.

## Vitamin D

Vitamin D has appeared frequently in Huberman's discussions, but it is another area where context matters. Vitamin D is essential, and correcting deficiency is important. That does not mean everyone benefits from pushing blood levels as high as possible.

Because vitamin D is fat soluble and excessive intake can cause toxicity, blood testing and clinician guidance are more useful than blindly copying someone else's dose.

## Tongkat ali

Tongkat ali, or *Eurycoma longifolia*, is one of the hormone-related supplements most closely associated with Huberman. He has discussed using it personally and has described doses in the **few-hundred-milligram-per-day range**, commonly around 400 mg in public discussions.

The proposed use is support for libido and free testosterone. There are human studies suggesting potential effects on stress, sexual function or testosterone in some populations, but the body of evidence is nowhere near as deep as it is for creatine.

Product quality is also a major issue with botanical extracts because the active-compound concentration can vary substantially among products.

## Fadogia agrestis: the page needs a much bigger caution than most stack lists give it

Fadogia agrestis is often included in online "Huberman testosterone stack" articles because he has discussed it in connection with luteinizing hormone and testosterone.

However, **human clinical evidence and long-term human safety data are extremely limited**. Animal research has raised toxicity concerns at higher doses, including possible effects on testicular tissue and other organs.

Huberman has himself emphasized blood testing when experimenting with hormone-related supplements. That is particularly important here.

We would not put fadogia in the same evidence tier as creatine, omega-3s or correcting a documented vitamin deficiency.

## Ashwagandha

Huberman has discussed ashwagandha as an adaptogen and stress-related supplement rather than as an essential part of one permanent daily stack.

Some research suggests certain ashwagandha preparations may help with stress or insomnia, but evidence is product-specific and studies are often small. Rare liver injury cases have also been reported, and it can interact with thyroid, blood-pressure, diabetes, sedative and immune-suppressing medications.

Source: [NCCIH — Ashwagandha: Usefulness and Safety](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ashwagandha)

## AG1 / Athletic Greens

AG1 has been a long-time Huberman Lab sponsor and Huberman has publicly discussed using it. That relationship should be kept separate from the scientific question of whether a greens powder is necessary.

A greens powder can be convenient, but it is not a replacement for fruits, vegetables, sufficient protein, fiber or a generally nutritious diet. People should also avoid assuming that dozens of ingredients at relatively small amounts automatically make a product superior to targeted supplementation based on an actual need.

## Electrolytes

Huberman frequently discusses hydration and electrolytes, especially sodium, in the context of neural function and exercise. Electrolyte powders such as LMNT have also appeared as podcast sponsors.

Whether someone needs a dedicated electrolyte supplement depends heavily on sweat rate, climate, exercise duration, diet and medical conditions. Many sedentary people already consume plenty of sodium, while endurance athletes training in heat may lose substantial amounts through sweat.

## Supplements Huberman has discussed are not automatically "Huberman's stack"

This is where many online lists go wrong. Huberman Lab has covered compounds such as lion's mane, bacopa, uridine, CoQ10, PQQ, shilajit, maca, L-carnitine, amino acids and many others. Their appearance in an episode should not be interpreted as proof that Huberman personally takes all of them.

For accuracy, we use three buckets:

1. **Personal use:** Huberman has explicitly said he takes or has taken it.
2. **Protocol/tool:** Huberman has described it as something people may consider for a defined goal.
3. **Discussion only:** the supplement came up in scientific or guest conversation without becoming a blanket recommendation.

That framework is much more useful than treating every molecule ever mentioned on the podcast as one giant daily stack.

## What would a simplified Huberman-style stack look like?

If you strip away the internet's tendency to turn every podcast mention into a shopping list, the practical framework becomes much simpler.

For a healthy adult, it might look more like this:

- Fix sleep schedule, morning light exposure, exercise and nutrition first.
- Correct actual nutrient deficiencies when they exist.
- Consider creatine if strength, power or possibly cognitive support are goals.
- Get sufficient EPA/DHA from fish or a supplement if dietary intake is low.
- Use goal-specific tools such as Alpha-GPC or sleep supplements selectively rather than automatically.
- Treat hormone-oriented botanicals as higher-uncertainty interventions that deserve more caution, better sourcing and often bloodwork.

That is much closer to Huberman's stated philosophy than purchasing 15 bottles and taking them all indefinitely.

## Safety: do not copy someone else's supplement stack blindly

Huberman regularly reminds listeners that supplement safety depends on the person, dose, medications, health conditions and product quality.

Extra caution is warranted if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, have kidney or liver disease, take prescription medications, have a cardiovascular condition, use anticoagulants, have a thyroid disorder, or are experimenting with supplements intended to alter hormones.

Supplements are also regulated differently from prescription drugs in the United States. Third-party testing can reduce — but not completely eliminate — the risk of contamination, adulteration or inaccurate labeling.

## Frequently asked questions

### What supplements does Andrew Huberman take every day?

There is no single permanent public list that should be treated as his unchanging daily stack. Creatine is one of the supplements he has clearly described using consistently. He has also publicly discussed omega-3s and several sleep and hormone-related supplements, but frequency and exact protocols have changed over time.

### What is Andrew Huberman's sleep stack?

The best-known combination is magnesium threonate or bisglycinate, apigenin and L-theanine before bed. He has also discussed myo-inositol, glycine and GABA for specific situations. He does not frame all of these as mandatory every night.

### How much creatine does Andrew Huberman take?

Huberman has said a common dose is 5 g per day and that he personally takes about 10 g per day at roughly 100 kg / 220 lb body weight. Creatine monohydrate is the form he discusses most often.

### How much Alpha-GPC does Huberman take?

He has described taking about 300 mg before workouts or focused cognitive work, while noting that 300–600 mg is a commonly discussed range. He has also said he tends to use it several times per week rather than necessarily every day.

### Does Andrew Huberman take tongkat ali?

He has publicly discussed personal use of tongkat ali and has described doses around the few-hundred-milligram range. That should not be interpreted as a recommendation for every listener, especially given the more limited evidence base compared with supplements such as creatine.

### Does Huberman still recommend fadogia agrestis?

It is safer to say that he has **discussed and historically used** fadogia rather than presenting it as an uncomplicated universal recommendation. Human safety evidence is limited, and animal toxicity findings make this one of the more caution-worthy items associated with his supplement discussions.

### Does Andrew Huberman recommend melatonin?

He is generally cautious about routine melatonin use for otherwise healthy adults and tends to emphasize behavioral sleep tools and other approaches first. Melatonin can still have legitimate circadian and clinical uses.

### Is Andrew Huberman's supplement stack scientifically proven?

No. The evidence ranges from strong for certain outcomes — such as creatine for strength and power — to preliminary or limited for others. Huberman often discusses mechanistic evidence and early research, which is not the same thing as proof of meaningful benefit in healthy humans.

## Bottom line

The most useful lesson from Andrew Huberman's supplement discussions is not the size of the stack. It is the idea of matching a tool to a specific problem while keeping the evidence, dose, side effects and alternatives in view.

**Higher-confidence options:** creatine monohydrate, correcting documented deficiencies, and obtaining adequate omega-3 intake.

**More goal-specific options:** magnesium, apigenin, L-theanine and Alpha-GPC.

**Higher-uncertainty options that deserve more caution:** tongkat ali, fadogia agrestis and other hormone-oriented botanicals.
